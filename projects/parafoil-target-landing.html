PROJECT 04 · AEROSPACE · RECOVERY · GUIDANCE

Guided Parafoil Recovery

Design study for targeted recovery of CAN-7USAT using an actively steered ram-air parafoil. The work was carried out for the college Rocketry & CanSat Club and later submitted to IN-SPACe for CDR consideration.

SKILLS

AEROSPACEPARAFOILGNCCAN-7USATCDR STUDY

Problem

The baseline hemispherical main chute provides passive descent but no control over final landing position. Wind therefore determines the landing footprint during the main-descent phase.

Proposed system

Replace the main chute at the existing 600 m trigger with a 0.40 m², 5-cell ram-air parafoil. Differential brake-line deflection provides heading control; symmetric brake input is reserved for flare.

Guidance

GPS position and course are converted to local east/north offsets and target bearing. A proportional bearing-error controller commands differential brake input at 5 Hz, with saturation and a low-altitude flare override.

Integration

The existing GPS, IMU and Teensy 4.1 flight computer are retained. Two PWM outputs are added for brake servos; the flight-state machine gains GUIDED GLIDE and FLARE states.

Aerodynamic sizing

The trim condition was sized from weight equilibrium:

V = √(2W / (ρ S CL)) = 7.81 m/s

With the selected aerodynamic coefficients:

L/D = CL / CD = 3.00
γ = atan(CD / CL) = 18.4°

Velocity components then give 7.41 m/s horizontal velocity and 2.47 m/s vertical sink rate. Three planform areas were compared. The 0.30 m² option exceeded the 3 m/s descent-rate limit; 0.50 m² reduced sink rate further but increased pack volume and reduced wing loading. The 0.40 m² configuration was retained as the baseline.

Glide range

Using a 15 m altitude reserve for the final flare, the usable descent height is 585 m.

t = 585 / 2.47 = 236.8 s
Range = 585 × 3.0 = 1,755 m

This is the theoretical no-wind range for a straight, full-glide trajectory. It is not treated as a guaranteed circular landing radius; available maneuvering margin depends on deployment geometry, wind and the time required for final approach.

Guidance and control

For the short operating range, GPS coordinates are converted using an equirectangular local-frame approximation. Target bearing is calculated with atan2 of the east and north offsets. Heading error is wrapped to ±180° and passed through a proportional controller:

e = bearingtarget − headingcurrent
u = Kp · e

The command is limited to a safe single-side brake deflection. Within approximately 30 m of the target, or below approximately 20 m AGL, the controller switches to symmetric flare. The design prioritises the descent-rate constraint over target tracking.

Worked case: a 190.4 m horizontal correction at a 59.1° target bearing requires approximately 25.7 s of transit and 63.5 m of altitude expenditure at the design-point velocities.

Actuation

Without wind-tunnel or tow-test data, the initial brake-line load was estimated conservatively as 15% of vehicle weight.

Fbrake ≈ 0.15W = 1.55 N
τ = Fbraker = 0.0155 N·m = 0.158 kgf·cm

A 10 mm servo horn was assumed. The existing MG90S, rated at approximately 1.8 kgf·cm at 4.8 V, provides more than 11× the estimated static torque requirement. The paper treats this margin as provisional because actual line loads require physical testing.

Mechanical integration and mass

The proposed deployment retains the existing recovery-bay latch but replaces dual-spring ejection with pilot-chute-assisted extraction. The pilot chute pulls the packed parafoil and lines clear of the bay in sequence, reducing the likelihood of line entanglement during deployment.

Risk and verification

The main parafoil-specific risks identified were partial inflation, line entanglement, guidance-induced unsafe trajectories, higher touchdown horizontal velocity, GPS loss and brake-servo failure. Mitigations include staged extraction, static inflation testing, brake-neutral GPS-loss fallback, hard-coded flare logic and neutral-return brake routing.

Verification was structured as a staged campaign: static canopy inflation, bench brake actuation, low-altitude guided drop, full-altitude drop, GPS-loss testing and flare-timing verification. The study recommends retaining the hemispherical main chute as the flight-qualified fallback until repeated drop tests demonstrate reliable inflation and controlled landing.
